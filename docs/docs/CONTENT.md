# Marketing Site Playbook: Copy, SEO/AEO & Conversion Standards

**Purpose:** working reference for building and auditing our own marketing site — homepage, all 8 service pages, About/Team, Contact. Two layers: **Part 1–2** are principles decided once; **Part 3–5** apply those principles by page type and by workstream. When writing or reviewing a page, start in Part 3 and follow the pointer back to the principle if you need the reasoning.

**How to read the tags:** each finding is tagged with its source and confidence. `[Primary]` = direct from the authoritative body itself (Google, NN/g, Baymard, Gartner, FTC). `[Corroborated]` = independent research houses agree (CXL, VWO, HubSpot's own data). `[Directional]` = credible but commercial/self-interested source, or single-study — treat as a strong signal, not gospel. Sections marked **⟳ Refresh** move fast and should be re-verified periodically; everything else is behavior- or law-based and stable for years.

## PART 1 — OPERATING PRINCIPLES
*Stable. The handful of truths that apply to every page, regardless of topic.*

### 1.1 People scan. They do not read.
`[Primary — NN/g]`
- 79% of users scan a new page; only 16% read word-by-word. On average, a visitor reads 20–28% of the words on a page.
- Eyes move in an **F-pattern**: two horizontal sweeps across the top, then a vertical scan down the left side. Content on the right side of a page is frequently missed entirely. The first two words of a line get disproportionately more attention than the third word onward.
- **Directive:** front-load every subhead, paragraph, and bullet with the word that carries the meaning. Never open with a throat-clearing phrase. Bold key terms. Use bullets/numbers for any list or process. One idea per paragraph — readers skip any second idea that isn't caught by the first few words.
- This holds on mobile too, with one addition: mobile users skip long intros even more aggressively. Operating rule: *if in doubt, leave it out.*

### 1.2 Plain language is not dumbing down — it's what experts want too.
`[Primary — NN/g]`
- Even highly educated, domain-expert readers prefer succinct, scannable writing over dense or jargon-heavy copy. This isn't a concession to a less sophisticated audience — it's universal.
- This directly validates the firm's existing "no jargon" voice standard. It's not just a style preference — it's the format the research says works.
- **Directive:** informative headlines, bulleted lists over paragraphs, generous white space, inverted-pyramid structure (conclusion first, supporting detail after).

### 1.3 The fold is real, but the actual danger is looking finished, not being short.
`[Primary — NN/g]`
- Users do scroll — more than they used to (scroll depth has risen over the past decade) — but still spend disproportionate time above the fold.
- The real failure mode is the **"illusion of completeness"**: if the first screen looks visually complete, a large share of users won't realize there's more below and will act on incomplete information (or bounce).
- **Directive:** don't cram everything above the fold, and don't end the first screen on a clean visual "stop" (a hard horizontal line, a tidy closed composition). Let something visibly continue — an image, a sentence, a section — so the page reads as "more below," not "done."

### 1.4 Trust, for a firm with no track record yet, comes from four durable levers.
`[Primary — NN/g, 1999, reconfirmed cross-culturally through 2020]`
1. **Design quality** — the site itself has to look competent; this is a marketing agency's own storefront.
2. **Up-front disclosure** — real names, real process, real "here's exactly what you get," stated plainly, not vaguely.
3. **Comprehensive, current content** — depth and visible freshness beat volume.
4. **Connection to the rest of the web** — external mentions, press, partner references. (This also ties directly into the AEO strategy in Part 2.4 — it's not a separate concern.)
- All four are things we can act on **before** we have a client roster. This is the antidote to "we don't have case studies yet."

### 1.5 Real photography outperforms stock, measurably.
`[Corroborated — CXL / VWO, citing controlled tests and peer-reviewed research]`
- A real photo of an actual person/client outperformed a top-performing stock photo by ~35% in a controlled test.
- Human, face-focused photos increase first-impression trust — but only if genuinely real. Visitors detect stock photography, and it actively *reduces* trust rather than being neutral.
- **Directive:** real founder/team photos over polished stock imagery, even if simpler. Highest-leverage, lowest-cost trust move available pre-portfolio.

### 1.6 Color psychology is mostly myth. Contrast is not.
`[Corroborated — multiple independent debunkings, tracing the popular stats to weak sourcing]`
- Claims like "blue means trust" or "red drives urgency" don't hold up under scrutiny — the underlying research is sparse, contradictory, and often mis-cited (the famous "90% of judgments are based on color" statistic traces back to a literature review, not a controlled experiment).
- What **does** hold up: an element that visually contrasts with its surroundings gets recognized and recalled significantly better than one that blends in.
- **Directive:** pick a distinctive, brand-consistent palette and stop there. Make CTAs stand out through contrast against their surrounding elements — not because of an assumed color-emotion rule.

### 1.7 Social proof works when it's specific, not when it exists.
`[Primary — CXL original eye-tracking research]`
- Different types of social proof don't differ much in how quickly they grab attention, but differ a lot in how long people engage and what they recall afterward.
- What performs: relevant to the visitor's specific situation, credible, **visual** (a photo measurably increases recall), and **specific** (a number beats a generalization).
- **Directive:** when testimonials start coming in, a one-line quote with a name, role, photo, and one specific result will outperform a paragraph of vague praise. Don't publish "great to work with!" — either get a specific line or don't use it yet.

## PART 2 — STRATEGIC / SITE-WIDE DECISIONS
*Decided once, apply everywhere. Not page-level copy tactics — these shape resourcing and structure.*

### 2.1 Navigation: recognition beats cleverness
`[Primary — NN/g + Baymard]`
- Specific, literal nav labels outperform clever or vague ones. "Explore" and "Learn More" fail in usability testing because they tell the user nothing about what's behind the click.
- Mobile hamburger icons are frequently overlooked — pair the icon with the word "Menu."
- Baymard's 2025 benchmark found 95% of sites fail to visually indicate which nav item corresponds to the current page — the single most common navigation failure measured.
- **Directive:** name all 8 service nav items literally (the service itself, not a branded/clever name for it). Visually highlight whichever page the visitor is currently on.

### 2.2 The B2B buying reality: hybrid, not self-service-only
`[Primary — Gartner]`
- ~75% of B2B buyers prefer a rep-free experience where possible, and buyers spend only ~17% of total purchase time actually meeting with vendors — the large majority of the journey is independent research.
- But: self-service-only purchases correlate with **higher buyer regret**. The winning model is hybrid — strong standalone content plus an easy, low-friction path to a human.
- **Directive:** every service page must be able to do real selling on its own (enough depth that a visitor can self-qualify), but must also make talking to a person trivially easy. Neither "content-only" nor "just call us" is sufficient alone.

### 2.3 AEO is not a technical checklist — Google says so directly
`[Primary — Google Search Central, official documentation]` **⟳ Refresh every 6–12 months**
- Google's own position: optimizing for AI search *is* SEO, not a separate discipline with separate rules.
- Explicitly **not required**, despite being common blog advice: chunking content into short answer blocks, mandatory FAQ schema, llms.txt files, or rewriting content specifically "for AI." None of these move the needle per Google's own guidance — see Part 5 for the full myth list.
- What actually matters: **non-commodity content** — a genuine first-hand point of view, not a summary of common knowledge. (Google's own example: generic "7 Tips for First-Time Homebuyers" vs. a specific first-hand account of an unusual choice and its outcome.)
- **Directive:** every service page needs a real point of view grounded in how we actually work — not templated "we drive results" language that could belong to any agency.

### 2.4 AEO's other half: off-site presence matters more than on-site polish
`[Directional — Semrush/Ahrefs, large-sample commercial research]` **⟳ Refresh every 3–6 months — this space moves fast**
- A brand's own website accounts for only ~5–10% of the sources AI search tools actually cite when answering questions. The large majority of AI citations come from earned media — third-party press, mentions, coverage — not owned content.
- Domain authority (breadth of who links to and mentions you) predicts AI citation likelihood more than any single page's optimization.
- **Directive:** AI/search visibility is not something the website alone can win. A parallel earned-mentions/PR motion (local press, partner mentions, guest content) needs to run alongside the site build, not after it. This is a resourcing decision, not a copywriting task — flag it for whoever owns growth strategy.

### 2.5 Page speed is a monorepo-wide risk, not a per-page one
`[Primary — Google's own published web.dev case studies]`
- Real A/B evidence: a 31% LCP improvement (Vodafone Italy) produced 8% more sales; a full Core Web Vitals optimization pass (Rakuten 24) produced a 33% conversion increase. Bounce probability roughly triples between a 1-second and 5-second load time.
- Because all sites/pages live in one shared repo, one bloated dependency or unoptimized asset on any single page can degrade Core Web Vitals sitewide.
- **Directive:** Lighthouse/PageSpeed checks belong in the PR review step of the Windsurf/Devin workflow, not as a post-launch audit item.

## PART 3 — APPLIED PLAYBOOK, BY PAGE TYPE
*Short and directive. Deeper reasoning lives in Part 1–2; this section just says what to do.*

### 3.1 Homepage
- Above-the-fold headline + subhead must pass the 5-second test: what we do, who it's for, why us — answerable without scrolling.
- Don't end the hero on a clean visual stop (§1.3) — let it signal more below.
- Lead with the visitor's problem before describing our service (problem-first framing consistently outperforms feature-first framing in service businesses).
- One primary CTA, not several competing ones.
- Nav: literal 8-service labels, current page highlighted (§2.1).

### 3.2 Each of the 8 service pages (SEO/AEO, Analytics, Paid Ads, Branding, Content, Automation, Reputation, Website)
- Open with the buyer's problem in their language, not a definition of the service.
- Include a genuine first-hand point of view per page — how we actually approach it, including a real opinion or method detail a generic competitor page wouldn't have (§2.3).
- Structure for scanning: short paragraphs, bolded key terms, descriptive subheads that front-load the important word (§1.1).
- Standalone-sellable: enough depth that a self-directed buyer can evaluate us without talking to anyone (§2.2) — but with a low-friction contact path visible without excessive scrolling.
- No fabricated urgency or scarcity language (§5.1).

### 3.3 About / Team
- Real photography, not stock — highest-leverage, lowest-cost trust move available right now (§1.5).
- Up-front disclosure over polish: real names, real process, plain statements of how we work (§1.4).
- If using a portfolio before paid client work exists: passion projects, spec work, and pro-bono pieces are legitimate to show — frame honestly, don't imply paid engagements that didn't happen.

### 3.4 Contact / Forms
`[Primary — Baymard Institute]`
- Cut fields to the essential minimum — most forms display roughly twice as many fields as necessary, and trimming meaningfully lifts completion.
- Labels sit **above** fields, never inline/floating — disappearing inline labels cause users to lose track of what they're filling in mid-form.
- Primary CTA should be reachable in the mobile thumb zone (bottom-center of the screen), not just placed at the top of the page the way desktop convention suggests (§4.3).

### 3.5 Testimonials / Case studies (medium-term, once available)
- One specific line (name, role, photo, one real number) beats a paragraph of vague praise (§1.7).
- Relevance to the specific visitor's situation matters more than volume of testimonials.

## PART 4 — TECHNICAL EXECUTION LAYER
*Dev-facing. Belongs in Windsurf/Devin prompts and PR review, not in page copy.*

### 4.1 Core Web Vitals
- Treat as a merge-blocking check, not a post-launch audit, given the shared-repo risk (§2.5).

### 4.2 Accessibility (WCAG 2.1/2.2 AA)
`[Corroborated — multiple current legal/industry sources]`
- Courts consistently treat WCAG 2.1 AA as the de facto standard even without a finalized private-sector federal rule. Lawsuit volume is rising sharply year over year, and the majority of targets are small/mid-size businesses, not enterprises — this is not an "our size is safe" issue.
- Accessibility and SEO overlap substantially: semantic heading structure, alt text, and logical navigation serve screen readers and search crawlers simultaneously.
- Common, cheap-to-fix failures: color contrast, missing alt text, unlabeled form fields, weak keyboard navigation, uncaptioned video.
- **Directive:** AI-generated code does not reliably produce accessible markup by default. Add an explicit accessibility checklist item to coding prompts and PR review — don't assume Windsurf/Devin output is compliant without checking.

### 4.3 Mobile thumb zone
`[Primary — Steven Hoober's original field research, ~1,300 users observed]`
- Roughly half of mobile use is one-handed and thumb-driven. The screen has a green zone (bottom-center, easy reach), a yellow zone (mid-screen, requires stretching), and a red zone (top corners, hard to reach without repositioning the grip).
- **Directive:** primary mobile CTAs (contact, get a quote) belong low on the screen or in a sticky bottom bar — not just placed at the top of the hero.

## PART 5 — GUARDRAILS: EXPLICITLY REJECTED TACTICS
*Concentrated here on purpose — so a stray blog post or a loosely-prompted AI coding assistant doesn't reintroduce one of these later.*

### 5.1 Fake urgency / scarcity — legal exposure, not just bad taste
`[Primary — FTC]`
- Fake countdown timers, fabricated "X people are viewing this," and non-genuine scarcity claims are active FTC Section 5 enforcement targets. Design elements are evaluated cumulatively, so even individually-defensible tactics can stack into a compliance issue.
- **Rule:** never use urgency/scarcity language unless it is literally, verifiably true.

### 5.2 AEO myths, contradicted directly by Google
`[Primary — Google Search Central]`
- Chunking content into rigid 40–60 word answer blocks: not required, no ideal page length exists.
- Mandatory schema/FAQ markup for AI inclusion: not required (still fine for rich results generally, just not an AEO lever).
- llms.txt or similar AI-specific files: ignored entirely by Google Search.
- Rewriting content specifically "for AI phrasing": unnecessary — models already understand synonyms and intent.

### 5.3 Color psychology as a design rulebook
- No "this color converts better" claims in creative decisions — see §1.6. Contrast, not hue, is the defensible lever.

### 5.4 Unverifiable conversion statistics
- Specific claims like "PAS-structured pages convert 22% higher" or "headlines under 11 words convert 17% better" were investigated during research and traced to a low-authority content-mill source with no corroboration. Not used anywhere in this document, and shouldn't be cited elsewhere without independent verification.
